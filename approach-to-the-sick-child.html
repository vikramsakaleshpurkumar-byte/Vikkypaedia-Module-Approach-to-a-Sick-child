01

Why children die, and where the module fits

Epidemiology, the deterioration continuum, and the case for a structured approach · ~1.5 h

›

A child who dies in hospital usually does so within 24 hours of arrival, from a small number of physiological end-points, after a period during which someone could have noticed. This module exists because of that sentence.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  • UG State the leading causes of under-5 and 5–19 year mortality globally and in India, and explain why most are preventable at the point of first contact.
  • UG Describe the deterioration continuum from well → compensated → decompensated → arrest, and name the two final common pathways to paediatric cardiac arrest.
  • PG Critique the evidence for structured recognition systems (ETAT, PEWS, rapid response teams) including where trials have been negative, and explain the implications for your own hospital.
  • PG Distinguish diagnostic error from recognition failure and locate each in a systems framework.

1.1 The mortality picture Must

Under-5 mortality has fallen dramatically over three decades, but the remaining deaths concentrate into a short list. Globally the largest contributors beyond the neonatal period are pneumonia, diarrhoea, malaria, injuries and malnutrition-associated infection; within the neonatal period, prematurity, intrapartum-related events (birth asphyxia) and neonatal infection dominate. In India the neonatal period now accounts for the majority of under-5 deaths, which is why Unit 8 (the sick young infant) is not an optional extra but a core clinical skill for anyone who sees children.

Two facts should shape how you read the rest of this module:

  1. Death clusters early. A large share of paediatric in-hospital deaths occur within the first 24 hours of admission. The window in which recognition changes outcome is measured in minutes to hours, not days.
  2. The pathway is respiratory, not cardiac. Unlike adults, children rarely have a primary arrhythmic arrest. Paediatric cardiac arrest is usually the end of a progressive respiratory or circulatory failure. This is the single most important physiological difference driving paediatric emergency practice — and it is good news, because it means there is a long, visible, interruptible runway before arrest.
India lens

First contact for a sick child in India is frequently not a paediatrician: it is an ASHA, an ANM, a PHC medical officer, a general practitioner, or a private clinic. The IMNCI and F-IMNCI programmes exist precisely to push structured recognition down to that level. Any module that teaches only PICU-grade assessment is teaching the wrong end of the pathway — recognition at first contact and safe referral save far more lives than any single ICU intervention.

1.2 The deterioration continuum Must

THE RUNWAY WELL │ (insult: infection, trauma, obstruction, toxin, metabolic) ▼ COMPENSATED ── tachycardia, tachypnoea, narrow pulse pressure, │ anxious or clingy, cool peripheries, normal BP │ ▸ NORMAL BLOOD PRESSURE. This is where you win. ▼ DECOMPENSATED ── hypotension, altered sensorium, bradypnoea, │ mottling, weak central pulses │ ▸ Hours of physiological credit already spent. ▼ PERI-ARREST ── bradycardia, agonal breathing, unresponsive │ ▼ ARREST ── usually asystole/PEA, survival poor

The clinically decisive point: a child's blood pressure is a late and treacherous sign. Children maintain blood pressure through vigorous tachycardia and vasoconstriction until compensation abruptly fails. A normal blood pressure in a tachycardic, cold, unwell child is not reassurance — it is a compensating child, and compensation is a countdown.

Classic pitfall

"BP was 96/60, so I wasn't worried." Hypotension in a child is a pre-terminal sign. By the time it appears, roughly 30–40% of circulating volume may already be lost in haemorrhagic shock. Treat the tachycardia, the capillary refill and the sensorium — not the number on the cuff.

1.3 Recognition failure vs diagnostic error Good

These are different problems with different fixes, and conflating them wastes effort.

Failure typeWhat happenedWhat fixes it
Recognition failureThe abnormal physiology was present and documentable but nobody registered it as abnormal, or registered it but did not escalate.Structured assessment (PAT/ETAT), track-and-trigger scores, mandated escalation criteria, empowering nurses and parents to escalate.
Diagnostic errorThe physiology was noticed; the underlying cause was misattributed (e.g. DKA called gastroenteritis; myocarditis called asthma).Illness scripts, forced differential generation, diagnostic time-outs, cognitive forcing strategies, follow-up loops.
Execution failureCorrectly recognised and diagnosed, but the intervention was late, wrong-dose, or unavailable.Checklists, pre-calculated dose charts, simulation, drug and oxygen supply chains.

Most of Part A and B of this module attacks recognition failure. Part D attacks diagnostic error. Part E attacks execution failure. Deciding which of the three is your department's actual problem is a worthwhile first act of quality improvement.

1.4 Does structured recognition actually work? Nice PG

Evidence note — read the negative trials too

ETAT / IMNCI in low-resource hospitals: introduction of structured emergency triage has been associated with substantial reductions in in-hospital paediatric mortality in several African and Asian implementations. The mechanism is simple — a queue is replaced by a triage decision.

PEWS in well-resourced hospitals: the EPOCH cluster-randomised trial (Parshuram et al., JAMA 2018; 21 hospitals, 7 countries, >144,000 patients) found that the Bedside Paediatric Early Warning System did not reduce all-cause hospital mortality compared with usual care. Later systematic reviews suggest PEWS may reduce critical deterioration events without a demonstrable mortality signal in settings that already have good baseline surveillance.

How to reconcile these: a track-and-trigger tool adds value in proportion to the surveillance gap it fills. In a tertiary PICU-adjacent ward with 1:2 nursing and a rapid response team, the marginal gain is small. In a 60-bed district paediatric ward with two night nurses and no formal triage, the same tool can be transformative. Do not import a negative trial's conclusion across a resource gradient. This is a habit of critical appraisal worth generalising well beyond PEWS.

Q1. A 3-year-old with 3 days of fever and poor feeding: HR 168, RR 46, BP 98/62, CRT 4 s, cool knees, irritable but consolable. Which statement is most accurate?

Compensated shock. Tachycardia + delayed capillary refill + cool peripheries + altered behaviour = inadequate perfusion, with blood pressure still maintained by compensation. Fever raises heart rate roughly 10 beats/min per °C — nowhere near enough to explain 168 with these perfusion findings. Lactate supports but never gates the diagnosis; shock is clinical.

Q2. Why is paediatric cardiac arrest usually asystole or PEA rather than ventricular fibrillation?

Progressive hypoxia/shock. Structural heart disease and channelopathies can produce shockable rhythms in children, so option 1 is false — but the dominant pathway is asphyxial. This is why the therapeutic priority is airway, oxygenation and perfusion long before it is electricity, and why prevention beats resuscitation in paediatrics far more than in adult practice.

The sixty-second assessment

Paediatric Assessment Triangle, WHO ETAT triage, and track-and-trigger scores · ~2 h

›

Before you touch the child, before the thermometer, before the history — you look and you listen from the doorway. Done properly this takes under a minute, requires no equipment, and decides the next hour.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Perform a Paediatric Assessment Triangle and convert it into one of six physiological categories.
  • UG Apply WHO ETAT to sort any child into Emergency / Priority / Queue within 60 seconds and name all emergency signs from memory.
  • UG Recognise the age-specific normal ranges for heart rate and respiratory rate and use them without a chart when necessary.
  • PG Select and defend an appropriate track-and-trigger tool for a given clinical setting.

2.1 The Paediatric Assessment Triangle (PAT) Must

The PAT is a purely observational, hands-off, cross-cultural instrument. Its three limbs are Appearance, Work of breathing and Circulation to skin. It answers two questions: how sick? and which system? — before any history or investigation.

LimbMnemonicWhat you are looking for

Work of breathing—Abnormal sounds audible without a stethoscope (stridor, grunt, wheeze) · abnormal position (tripod, sniffing, refusing to lie flat) · retractions (subcostal, intercostal, suprasternal) · nasal flaring · head bobbing in infants

Teaching pearl

"An abnormal Appearance with normal breathing and normal circulation" is the row most often missed on wards. That child has a brain or a metabolic problem — hypoglycaemia, meningitis/encephalitis, poisoning, non-convulsive status, intussusception with lethargy, or an inborn error decompensating. Check a glucose before you check anything else.

2.2 WHO ETAT — Emergency Triage Assessment and Treatment Must

ETAT is the WHO's triage system for facilities where children arrive in numbers and queue. It sorts every arriving child into three groups using the mnemonic ABCD-Coma-Convulsion-Dehydration. Its brilliance is that it is executable by a nurse or a trained health worker at the door, without investigations.

ETAT — EMERGENCY SIGNS ("ABCD") → treat NOW, do not queue Airway Obstructed or absent breathing Breathing Central cyanosis · severe respiratory distress Circulation Cold hands WITH capillary refill >3 s AND weak, fast pulse Coma Unconscious / not alert (AVPU ≤ P) Convulsion Convulsing now Dehydration (in a child with diarrhoea) — any two of: lethargy/unconsciousness, sunken eyes, very slow skin pinch, unable to drink PRIORITY SIGNS ("3TPR-MOB") → move to front of queue Tiny baby (< 2 months) Temperature (very hot) Trauma / urgent surgical Pallor (severe) Poisoning Pain (severe) Respiratory distress Restless / irritable / lethargic Referral (urgent) Malnutrition (visible severe wasting) Oedema of both feet Burns (major) NON-URGENT → wait in queue
Evidence note

WHO's Paediatric emergency triage, assessment and treatment: care of critically-ill children (2016) updated the 2005 ETAT materials and the 2013 second edition of the Pocket Book of Hospital Care for Children, with specific recommendations on oxygen therapy, flow rates and humidification in children with emergency signs. Where implemented with training and supervision, ETAT has repeatedly been associated with reductions in early in-hospital mortality. Note the qualifier: with training and supervision. A triage form pinned to a wall is not a triage system.

Pitfall — the crying child

Count respiratory rate over a full 60 seconds in a calm child. A screaming toddler's rate is uninterpretable, and a rate counted over 15 seconds and multiplied by four is unreliable in infants with periodic breathing. If the child settles on the parent's shoulder, count then.

2.4 Track-and-trigger: PEWS and its cousins Good PG

Paediatric Early Warning Scores convert repeated observations into an escalation trigger. Common variants weight behaviour, cardiovascular and respiratory domains (Brighton PEWS), or use continuous physiological items (Bedside PEWS). Whichever tool, three design features matter more than the score itself:

  1. A mandated response, not just a number. A score of 7 must summon someone with a defined skill set within a defined time.
  2. A parent/nurse override. "I am worried about this child" must be a legitimate trigger independent of the score. Carer concern is a recognised, independently predictive signal.
  3. Feedback loops. Every arrest and unplanned PICU transfer reviewed against the preceding 12 hours of observations.
India lens — a realistic minimum

A full electronic PEWS is unrealistic in most Indian district hospitals. A defensible minimum: ETAT triage at the door by a trained nurse, a colour-coded observation chart with printed age-specific abnormal thresholds, a written escalation ladder pinned at the nursing station, and a standing instruction that any child with an emergency sign is seen by a doctor immediately. That package costs almost nothing and addresses the recognition gap directly.

Q1. Applying ETAT at the door: a 14-month-old with 4 days of diarrhoea is lethargic, has sunken eyes and a very slow skin pinch, but warm hands and capillary refill of 2 s. Correct triage category?

Emergency. ETAT's "D" is severe dehydration in a child with diarrhoea, defined by any two of lethargy/unconsciousness, sunken eyes, very slow skin pinch, or inability to drink. This child has three. Warm hands do not downgrade it — hypovolaemia can be severe before peripheral shutdown in dehydration, and the child is on the edge of hypovolaemic shock.

Q2. A 4-year-old sits upright, refuses to lie down, has audible inspiratory stridor and suprasternal retractions, is alert and interacting, and has normal skin colour. PAT category?

Respiratory distress. Appearance is intact, so the brain is still being perfused and oxygenated — this child is compensating. The critical management corollary: do not force this child supine and do not distress them. Position of comfort on the parent's lap, blow-by oxygen, and get senior/airway help before any intervention that might precipitate complete obstruction.

03

Why children crash late and fast

Developmental physiology, compensatory reserve, and clinical reasoning under uncertainty · ~2 h

›

Every difference between adult and paediatric emergency practice traces back to a handful of anatomical and physiological facts. Learn the facts and the protocols become obvious rather than memorised.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Explain, from first principles, why airway obstruction, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia and hypothermia develop faster in small children.
  • UG Predict how cardiac output is generated in infants and why bradycardia is an emergency.
  • PG Apply dual-process reasoning and named debiasing strategies to a paediatric emergency.

3.1 Airway Must

  • Large occiput in infants flexes the neck when supine → obstruction. Correct with a shoulder roll, aim for the neutral position in infants and the sniffing position in older children.
  • Relatively large tongue, small mandible → posterior tongue obstruction is the commonest cause of airway compromise in an obtunded child, and a simple jaw thrust often fixes it entirely.
  • Narrow airways. Resistance to laminar flow is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the radius. In a 4 mm infant trachea, 1 mm of circumferential oedema cuts the radius by half and multiplies resistance roughly sixteen-fold. The same 1 mm in an adult is trivial. This one equation explains croup, bronchiolitis, and why a crying child with an inflamed airway can decompensate in seconds.
  • Obligate nasal breathing in young infants → nasal blockage alone can cause significant distress and feeding failure. Saline drops and suction are real therapy, not a placebo.

3.2 Breathing Must

  • Higher metabolic rate, higher oxygen consumption (roughly 6–8 mL/kg/min in infants vs 3–4 in adults) with proportionally smaller functional residual capacity → desaturation on apnoea is dramatically faster. Pre-oxygenation before intubation buys you seconds, not minutes.
  • Compliant chest wall, horizontal ribs, diaphragm-dependent breathing → retractions are prominent but ventilation is inefficient; abdominal distension genuinely splints the diaphragm (hence gastric decompression during resuscitation).
  • Fatigue-prone diaphragm with fewer type I fibres in infants → sustained increased work of breathing ends in exhaustion. A falling respiratory rate in a previously tachypnoeic child is a sign of imminent respiratory arrest, not improvement.
The most dangerous sign in paediatrics

A tiring child looks calmer. Retractions lessen, the respiratory rate falls, the child becomes quiet and drowsy, and inexperienced observers relax. Silence, a falling rate and a settling child in the context of prior severe distress is pre-arrest. Reassess, don't relax.

3.3 Circulation Must

  • Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate. Infant myocardium has less contractile tissue and limited ability to increase stroke volume → cardiac output is largely rate-dependent. Consequence: bradycardia in an infant is functional cardiac arrest, and the commonest cause is hypoxia. Treat bradycardia with oxygen and ventilation first; atropine is for vagally-mediated bradycardia, not asphyxial.
  • Vigorous vasoconstriction preserves blood pressure at the expense of peripheral perfusion → cold peripheries, delayed capillary refill and narrow pulse pressure appear long before hypotension.
  • Small absolute blood volume (roughly 80 mL/kg in infants, 70 mL/kg in children) → a 10 kg child has ~800 mL total. Apparently modest bleeding or third-spacing is proportionally catastrophic. So is repeated blood sampling in a neonate.

A usable habit: the diagnostic time-out

Before leaving any sick child: (1) What is my diagnosis? (2) What doesn't fit? (3) What is the worst thing this could be, and have I excluded it? (4) What must happen if the child gets worse, and who does it? Thirty seconds. It catches more errors than any checklist you will be given.

Q1. A 6-month-old with bronchiolitis has had severe retractions for 6 hours. On review, retractions are less marked, RR has fallen from 70 to 34, and the infant is sleepy and not feeding. SpO₂ 92% on oxygen. Most appropriate interpretation?

Impending failure. The infant diaphragm fatigues. Falling respiratory rate plus reduced work of breathing plus a deteriorating conscious level, in a child with prior severe distress, is decompensation. The SpO₂ of 92% on oxygen is not reassuring — it tells you the oxygen is barely keeping up. This child needs respiratory support (high-flow/CPAP or ventilation), a blood gas, and senior review immediately.

Q2. A 2-month-old has a heart rate of 54/min and is poorly perfused. First intervention?

Oxygenate and ventilate. Paediatric bradycardia is hypoxic until proven otherwise, and infant cardiac output is rate-dependent — so bradycardia with poor perfusion is functionally an arrest state. Per PALS, if the heart rate remains under 60/min with poor perfusion despite effective oxygenation and ventilation, start chest compressions, then give epinephrine. Atropine is reserved for bradycardia of vagal or primary AV-block origin; adenosine treats SVT, not bradycardia.

Airway and Breathing

Recognising and relieving upper airway obstruction; respiratory distress vs failure; oxygen and support · ~2.5 h

›

Airway before breathing, breathing before circulation — not because the others matter less, but because failure at each step makes the next impossible to fix.

Learning outcomes

  • UG Distinguish upper from lower airway obstruction and from lung parenchymal and disordered-control breathing patterns.

Grunting

Grunting is expiration against a partly closed glottis — the child generating their own PEEP. In a child it signals alveolar collapse and is a marker of significant parenchymal disease or, in the young infant, of sepsis, pain or acidosis. Never dismiss a grunting child.

4.3 Opening and supporting the airway Must

STEPWISE AIRWAY 1. POSITION Infant: neutral, shoulder roll. Child: sniffing. Trauma: jaw thrust with manual in-line stabilisation. 2. CLEAR Gentle suction of secretions/vomit under direct vision. Do NOT blind-finger-sweep. 3. ADJUNCT Oropharyngeal airway if unconscious with no gag — size: incisors to angle of mandible; insert the right way up in children (do not rotate 180° as in adults — risk of palatal trauma). Nasopharyngeal airway if gag present and no base-of-skull injury. 4. VENTILATE Bag–mask with reservoir, high-flow O2. E-C clamp grip. Watch CHEST RISE, not the bag. Rate ≈ 20–30/min (1 breath every 2–3 s) for infants and children in respiratory arrest with a pulse. 5. DECOMPRESS Nasogastric tube — gastric distension splints the diaphragm and causes regurgitation. 6. ESCALATE Supraglottic airway or tracheal intubation by a competent operator. Waveform capnography confirms.
Evidence note — PALS 2025 changes you must know
  • Ventilation rate raised. 20–30 breaths/min (one breath every 2–3 seconds) for infants and children with an advanced airway during CPR, and for respiratory arrest with a perfusing rhythm — a change from the older 1-breath-every-6-seconds guidance carried over from adults.
  • Cricoid pressure is not recommended during tracheal intubation in children.
  • Early epinephrine in non-shockable arrest is associated with better outcomes; in shockable rhythms, give epinephrine after the second defibrillation attempt.
  • Physiology-directed resuscitation is explicitly elevated — use invasive BP, capnography and real-time CPR feedback to individualise, rather than running the algorithm blind.
  • Post-arrest: target systolic BP above the 10th centile for age, and actively prevent hyperthermia.

Source: AHA/AAP 2025 Guidelines for CPR and ECC, Part 8 (Pediatric Advanced Life Support), Circulation 2025. The 2025 update was the first developed as an equal AHA–AAP partnership and the first major revision in five years.

India lens — oxygen as a system, not a drug

The commonest failure is not the prescription but the supply: empty cylinders, absent flow-splitters, missing humidification, non-functional concentrators, no pulse oximeter, no paediatric-size masks. WHO's 2016 ETAT update deliberately addressed oxygen flow rates and humidification for exactly this reason. Auditing "was oxygen available and correctly delivered" is often a higher-yield QI project in a district hospital than any clinical protocol change.

Supporting the failing airway — ideal
  • Piped oxygen and blended FiO₂; continuous pulse oximetry and waveform capnography on every supported child.
  • Stepwise escalation available: nasal prongs → HFNC (1–2 L/kg/min) → CPAP/NIV → intubation and lung-protective ventilation.
  • Blood gas guides the decision; a difficult-airway trolley, videolaryngoscope and an anaesthetist are reachable within minutes.
  • Decision to intubate can be elective and unhurried, made before exhaustion.
Supporting the failing airway — resource-constrained
  • Oxygen is the intervention. Concentrator or cylinder with a flow-splitter, a working pulse oximeter, and correctly sized masks and prongs — audit that these exist before the next sick child arrives.
  • Position of comfort on the parent's lap achieves much of what equipment does, and costs nothing.
  • Improvised bubble CPAP is a recognised, evidence-supported option for infants with severe pneumonia or bronchiolitis where ventilation is unavailable — but only with staff trained to run and monitor it.
  • If you cannot ventilate beyond bag–mask, the decision is when to transfer, not whether to intubate. Make it early, while the child is still compensating (Unit 15).
  • Intubating a child you cannot then ventilate or monitor converts a survivable illness into an arrest. Bag–mask ventilation done well is a legitimate destination, not a failure.

4.5 Escalation: HFNC, NIV, intubation Good PG

Choose by mechanism. HFNC helps where dead-space washout and mild distending pressure suffice (bronchiolitis, mild-moderate pneumonia). CPAP helps where the problem is alveolar collapse or upper airway collapse. BiPAP/NIV helps where the problem is inadequate tidal ventilation with hypercapnia. Intubation is for airway protection, refractory hypoxaemia or hypercapnia, exhaustion, shock unresponsive to resuscitation, and controlled transfer.

Paediatric RSI — a safe sequence
  1. Prepare: weight-based drug doses drawn and labelled; two suctions; correctly sized tube ± half-size above and below; laryngoscope checked; bougie/stylet; capnography connected; monitor; two operators; a spoken plan A/B/C for failed intubation.
  2. Tube size (uncuffed): (age/4) + 4 mm. Cuffed: (age/4) + 3.5 mm. Cuffed tubes are now acceptable in all ages including infants provided cuff pressure is monitored (< 20–25 cmH₂O). Depth: ≈ tube size × 3 cm at the lips.
  3. Pre-oxygenate and consider apnoeic oxygenation via nasal cannula. Desaturation is fast — the margin is seconds.
  4. Optimise physiology first: a shocked or acidotic child can arrest at induction. Resuscitate before you intubate where possible; choose a haemodynamically kind induction agent (e.g. ketamine) and have a push-dose vasopressor available.
  5. No cricoid pressure (PALS 2025).
  6. Confirm with waveform capnography plus bilateral chest rise and auscultation. Secure. Post-intubation chest radiograph. Then sedate and ventilate lung-protectively.
  7. DOPES for acute deterioration in a ventilated child: Displacement · Obstruction · Pneumothorax · Equipment failure · Stomach (distension).

Q1. A 2-year-old has sudden-onset coughing and stridor while eating peanuts, is distressed but coughing forcefully and speaking. Best immediate action?

Effective cough = do not interfere. An effective cough generates higher airway pressures than any manoeuvre you can apply. Back blows and thrusts are for an ineffective cough (silent, unable to speak or breathe). Blind finger sweeps risk impacting the object further. Keep the child calm, give oxygen if tolerated without distress, and arrange urgent bronchoscopy — with anaesthesia and ENT ready in case of decompensation.

Q2. During CPR in a 4-year-old with a tracheal tube in place, which ventilation rate matches the 2025 PALS guidance?

20–30/min. The 2025 AHA–AAP guidelines set 1 breath every 2–3 seconds for infants and children with an advanced airway during CPR, and the same rate for respiratory arrest with a pulse — reflecting evidence that paediatric arrest is predominantly asphyxial and that higher ventilation rates are associated with better outcomes. Compressions continue uninterrupted at 100–120/min with a compression fraction above 60%.

5.4 Fluid resuscitation — and the argument about it Must

The classical teaching: in decompensated shock, give isotonic crystalloid 20 mL/kg over 5–20 minutes, reassess after every bolus, and repeat as indicated up to 40–60 mL/kg in the first hour while looking for causes and starting vasoactive support if shock persists. In cardiogenic shock, in severe acute malnutrition, and in severe anaemia, the bolus is reduced or omitted.

Reassess after every bolus using the same four questions: Is the heart rate falling? Is the sensorium improving? Is perfusion improving? Has the liver enlarged, or have crackles or a gallop appeared? The last question is your stop signal.

Evidence note — FEAST and what it really means

The FEAST trial (Maitland et al., NEJM 2011; 3,170 febrile African children with impaired perfusion) randomised children to a 20–40 mL/kg bolus of saline or 5% albumin versus maintenance fluid only. Bolus therapy increased 48-hour mortality (absolute increase ≈ 3.3%), and the harm was consistent across shock definitions. Mechanistic re-analyses suggested cardiovascular collapse rather than fluid overload as the mode of death.

Read the setting carefully. FEAST was conducted in hospitals without mechanical ventilation or inotrope infusions, in children with malaria and severe anaemia, and it excluded gastroenteritis and severe malnutrition. Its most defensible reading is not "fluid is bad" but: aggressive bolus resuscitation is dangerous when you cannot support the airway and myocardium that the bolus may unmask.

Practical synthesis for an Indian district hospital: in a febrile child with impaired perfusion but no hypotension, and no capacity for ventilation or inotropes, prefer careful, smaller aliquots (10 mL/kg) with reassessment after each, early antimicrobials, correction of hypoglycaemia and anaemia, and early referral — rather than reflexive 60 mL/kg. In frank hypotensive shock, or in clear hypovolaemia from diarrhoea, fluid remains life-saving and should not be withheld.

India lens — three groups where the standard bolus is wrong
  1. Severe acute malnutrition: fluid overload and cardiac failure are real risks. WHO advises against routine rapid bolus in SAM; oral or nasogastric ReSoMal is preferred, and where shock is present with lethargy or unconsciousness the IV regimen is 15 mL/kg over one hour of a dextrose-containing solution, with frequent reassessment for hepatomegaly, rising pulse and respiratory rate, and puffiness. Covered fully in Unit 12.
  2. Severe anaemia (Hb often < 5 g/dL, common with malaria and nutritional deficiency): the problem is oxygen-carrying capacity, not volume. Transfuse; do not flood.
  3. Dengue: plasma leak physiology needs a specific, phase-dependent, titrated fluid strategy — over-resuscitation in the recovery phase causes pulmonary oedema. Follow the national dengue guidelines, not generic septic shock protocols.
The shocked child — ideal
  • Isotonic crystalloid in 10–20 mL/kg aliquots, reassessing after each, up to 40–60 mL/kg in the first hour if fluid-responsive.
  • Stop for hepatomegaly, crackles or a gallop; move to vasoactive infusion (adrenaline for cold shock, noradrenaline for warm) rather than more fluid.
  • Objective adjuncts guide titration: serial lactate, central venous saturation, echocardiography, invasive arterial pressure, urine output.
  • Intubation and ventilation available to unload the myocardium and secure the airway if fluid unmasks cardiac failure.
The shocked child — resource-constrained
  • Hypotensive shock, or clear hypovolaemia from diarrhoea or haemorrhage: fluid is life-saving. Give it — 10 mL/kg aliquots with reassessment after each.
  • Febrile child with impaired perfusion but a normal blood pressure: this is the FEAST population. Withhold the routine bolus. Give maintenance fluid, immediate antimicrobials, correct hypoglycaemia and severe anaemia, keep the child warm, and transfer.
  • You cannot rescue a child from the cardiovascular collapse a large bolus may precipitate if you have no ventilator and no inotrope. That is the whole argument.
  • Where an infusion pump is unavailable, low-dose dopamine through a peripheral or intraosseous line is the pragmatic vasoactive of last resort — with vigilant observation of the site.
  • Do the cheap things perfectly: oxygen, glucose, antibiotics within the hour, an accurate weight, temperature control, and a documented reassessment every 15 minutes. These carry most of the mortality benefit.

Q1. A 5-month-old presents with poor feeding, tachypnoea, HR 210, cool peripheries and CRT 4 s. Liver is palpable 4 cm below the costal margin and a gallop is audible. Best immediate fluid strategy?

Cautious volume, urgent cardiac assessment. Hepatomegaly plus gallop in a shocked infant points to cardiogenic shock — myocarditis, cardiomyopathy, SVT with heart failure, or a duct-dependent lesion. A standard 20 mL/kg bolus can precipitate pulmonary oedema and arrest. Get an ECG (a rate of 210 that is invariant with no beat-to-beat variability suggests SVT, which is treated with vagal manoeuvres/adenosine, not fluid), an echocardiogram if available, and start an inotrope. Withholding all fluid is also wrong if there is genuine hypovolaemia — the answer is titration, not abstinence.

Q2. You cannot obtain peripheral IV access in a decompensated shocked 2-year-old after two attempts and 90 seconds. Next step?

Intraosseous. IO access is fast, has a high first-pass success rate, and accepts every drug, fluid and blood product that a peripheral IV does — including vasopressors. It should be a reflex, not a last resort. Remember that gravity flow is inadequate: push fluid with a syringe or use a pressure bag, and flush the marrow space firmly before the first infusion.

7.1 The exposure survey Must

Undress fully, look everywhere, then cover and warm. The specific things a hurried examiner misses:

The Second Pediatric Acute Lung Injury Consensus Conference (2023) refined the definition of paediatric ARDS: onset within 7 days of a known insult, oedema not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload, new uni- or bilateral opacities on imaging consistent with parenchymal disease, with severity graded using P/F, S/F, oxygenation index (OI) or oxygen saturation index (OSI). Importantly, PALICC-2 introduced "possible PARDS" and "at risk for PARDS" categories, and permits diagnosis on non-invasive support and using pulse-oximetry-based indices — deliberate design choices that make the definition usable in resource-limited settings without arterial lines or CT.

Q1. A 4-year-old is drowsy with pinpoint pupils, copious oral secretions, wheeze, bradycardia and muscle fasciculations after playing in a field. Priority after airway and oxygen?

Atropine to drying, plus pralidoxime. This is a cholinergic toxidrome from organophosphate exposure. Children die of bronchorrhoea and respiratory failure, not of bradycardia — so the titration endpoint is clear chest and dry secretions, not pupil size or heart rate, and doses required may be far higher than usual. Pralidoxime reactivates acetylcholinesterase and is most useful early, before ageing of the enzyme. Wear gloves and decontaminate the skin and clothing to protect yourself and other staff.

Q2. A 2-year-old admitted with gastroenteritis and given hypotonic maintenance fluid develops a seizure on day 2. Sodium is 118 mmol/L. Best immediate management?

Hypertonic saline. Symptomatic hyponatraemia is a neurological emergency and will not respond to antiseizure medication until osmolality is corrected; a small, rapid rise of 4–6 mmol/L is usually enough to stop seizures. After that, correct slowly (≈ 8 mmol/L per 24 h) to avoid osmotic demyelination. The larger lesson: hypotonic maintenance fluids cause hospital-acquired hyponatraemia in children, and isotonic maintenance fluid is now the standard of care.

The sick young infant — resource-constrained

Q1. A 9-day-old is brought with poor feeding and lethargy. HR 190, RR 70, femoral pulses weak, liver 4 cm, SpO₂ 84% pre-ductal and 74% post-ductal, no improvement on 100% oxygen. Most appropriate immediate action?

Prostaglandin E1. Presentation at day 9, differential saturation, weak femoral pulses, hepatomegaly and failure to respond to oxygen point to a duct-dependent lesion with the arterial duct closing — a coarctation or interrupted aortic arch physiology. Prostaglandin reopens the duct and buys time for transfer to a cardiac centre. Anticipate apnoea, which is a recognised effect, and be ready to ventilate. Large fluid boluses in this physiology will worsen pulmonary oedema; give volume only in small, reassessed aliquots.

Q2. A 5-week-old has a rectal temperature of 38.4 °C and looks entirely well, feeding normally, with normal observations. Best approach?

Full evaluation and empirical antibiotics. Under 3 months — and particularly under 1 month — clinical appearance is an unreliable discriminator for serious bacterial infection, because immune immaturity means the infant can be bacteraemic while still feeding and interacting. NICE and WHO both place fever in this age group in the highest-risk category. Newer risk-stratification tools (Step-by-Step, PECARN, Rochester) can safely reduce lumbar punctures and admissions in selected infants over 21–28 days, but they require inflammatory markers including procalcitonin and do not apply to the youngest or to any infant who looks unwell.

Sepsis is the final common pathway for most preventable childhood deaths. It is also the condition where the gap between what guidelines recommend and what a district hospital can deliver is widest — which makes intelligent adaptation, not protocol recitation, the real skill.

9.1 Definitions have changed — know why Must

For two decades paediatric sepsis was defined as SIRS + suspected infection. That definition was abandoned because SIRS criteria are met by most children with a simple viral illness — they were sensitive to the point of uselessness and identified a population whose mortality bore no relation to the label.

The Phoenix criteria (JAMA, 2024)

The SCCM Pediatric Sepsis Definition Task Force derived and validated a data-driven score from more than three million paediatric encounters across five countries (Schlapbach et al. and Sanchez-Pinto et al., JAMA 2024).

  • Sepsis = suspected or confirmed infection + a Phoenix Sepsis Score ≥ 2, indicating life-threatening organ dysfunction.
  • The score assesses four organ systems: respiratory, cardiovascular, coagulation and neurological (0–3 points each; maximum 13).
  • Septic shock = sepsis with ≥ 1 cardiovascular point — that is, hypotension for age, lactate ≥ 5 mmol/L, or receipt of a vasoactive medication.
  • A research-only 8-organ extension, Phoenix-8, adds renal, hepatic, immunological and endocrine domains.

Why it matters clinically: Phoenix defines sepsis by organ dysfunction, not by inflammation. It is deliberately usable with widely available variables, which was an explicit design goal for applicability in resource-limited settings. It also abandons the term "severe sepsis" — under Phoenix, sepsis is the severe entity.

Phoenix is not a screening tool

Phoenix identifies children who already have organ dysfunction — it is a definition for diagnosis, audit and research. It is not a trigger to start treatment, because waiting for two Phoenix points means waiting for organ failure. Screening remains clinical and physiological: the unwell-looking child with fever or hypothermia, tachycardia, poor perfusion, altered behaviour or a rising early-warning score. Treat on suspicion; define on data.

Evidence note — Surviving Sepsis Campaign Pediatrics 2026

The 2026 update (Pediatric Critical Care Medicine / Intensive Care Medicine, 2026) was produced by 68 international experts from 13 organisations with six methodologists, and issued 61 statements: 5 strong recommendations, 24 conditional recommendations and 10 good-practice statements. Relative to the 2020 guideline, 20 recommendations are new, 13 updated, 6 reviewed unchanged and 22 carried forward by consensus. It incorporates the Phoenix organ-dysfunction framing of sepsis and septic shock.

The most important thing about it is a weakness, not a strength: only three of the 61 statements rest on high or moderate certainty evidence. Almost everything you will be asked to do for a septic child is a conditional recommendation built on low-certainty evidence. That is not a reason for nihilism — it is a reason to individualise, to reassess constantly, and to be sceptical of anyone who presents a sepsis bundle as settled science.

India lens — the health-system-differentiated recommendation

Since 2020 the Surviving Sepsis paediatric guidelines have explicitly separated advice for settings with and without intensive care availability, largely as a consequence of FEAST. The practical translation for a PHC/CHC or district hospital:

  • Do the cheap things perfectly: oxygen, glucose, early appropriate antibiotics, temperature control, correction of severe anaemia, an accurate weight, and a documented reassessment schedule. These carry most of the mortality benefit.
  • Be conservative with boluses in a febrile, non-hypotensive child when you cannot ventilate or infuse an inotrope.
  • Refer early and refer well — the decision to transfer should be made while the child is still compensating, not after decompensation (Unit 15).
  • Know your antibiogram. Empirical cover for community-acquired paediatric sepsis in India generally means a third-generation cephalosporin ± an aminoglycoside, with vancomycin added for suspected staphylococcal or toxic shock presentations and carbapenems reserved for known/likely ESBL or hospital-acquired infection — but the correct answer is local, not textbook. Add doxycycline where scrub typhus is plausible, and antimalarials where malaria is endemic.

Evidence note — PECARN FLUID trial

The long-standing belief that rapid or high-sodium fluid administration causes cerebral oedema in DKA was tested in the PECARN FLUID trial (Kuppermann et al., NEJM 2018; 1,389 episodes, factorial design of fast vs slow rehydration and 0.9% vs 0.45% saline). Neither the rate of administration nor the sodium content significantly affected neurological outcomes. This has relaxed the previously extreme fluid caution — but current ISPAD guidance still favours even, controlled deficit replacement over 24–48 hours, avoidance of insulin boluses, and delaying insulin until fluids are running. The mechanism of cerebral injury in DKA appears more related to the severity of the presenting illness itself than to treatment variables, which is an argument for earlier diagnosis of diabetes, not for aggressive fluid.

India lens

A large proportion of Indian children with type 1 diabetes present in DKA at first diagnosis, often after being treated for "gastroenteritis" or "abdominal pain". Check a capillary glucose in every child with unexplained tachypnoea, vomiting, abdominal pain or dehydration. Kussmaul breathing is repeatedly misread as pneumonia or asthma, and children are given bronchodilators while acidosis deepens. Recurrent DKA in a known diabetic child is usually a social and access problem — insulin cost, refrigeration, school support — not a clinical one, and treating it as purely medical guarantees readmission.

DKA — ideal
  • Insulin by syringe-pump infusion at 0.05–0.1 units/kg/h, started 1–2 hours after fluids; deficit replaced evenly over 24–48 h.
  • Hourly glucose and neurological observations; 2–4 hourly electrolytes, gas and ketones; potassium added once urine is passed.
  • Hyperosmolar therapy immediately available at the bedside for suspected cerebral oedema.
DKA and dehydration — resource-constrained
  • Oral rehydration is the headline act, not the fallback. WHO Plans A and B, with zinc for 14 days and continued feeding, resolve the overwhelming majority of diarrhoeal dehydration without a cannula. Under-use of ORS remains a bigger problem in India than incorrect IV therapy.
  • In DKA without an infusion pump, hourly subcutaneous or intramuscular short-acting insulin regimens are a recognised alternative — never a bolus, never intermittent large doses, and only with hourly glucose monitoring by glucometer.
  • Without laboratory potassium, be conservative: add potassium only once the child is passing urine, and watch for arrhythmia clinically.
  • Cerebral oedema is diagnosed and treated clinically. Headache, vomiting, bradycardia with rising blood pressure or falling conscious level → give hypertonic saline or mannitol immediately. Do not wait for imaging you may not have.
  • The prevention that matters most here is earlier diagnosis of diabetes: check a capillary glucose in every child with unexplained tachypnoea, vomiting, abdominal pain or dehydration, and stop treating Kussmaul breathing as asthma.

Q1. An 11-year-old with new DKA (pH 7.05) has had 10 mL/kg saline and an insulin infusion started at hour 2. At hour 6 he develops a headache, vomits twice, and his heart rate falls from 120 to 74 with a rising blood pressure. Next action?

Treat immediately on clinical grounds. Headache, vomiting, bradycardia with hypertension and falling conscious level in DKA is cerebral oedema until proven otherwise, and it is the leading cause of DKA mortality in children. Imaging must never delay hyperosmolar therapy — a normal early CT does not exclude it. Bicarbonate is contraindicated: it is independently associated with cerebral injury in paediatric DKA.

Q2. A 14-month-old with severe dehydration from acute watery diarrhoea, weight 9 kg, no malnutrition. According to WHO Plan C, the correct initial IV regimen is:

Dextrose 5% is not a resuscitation fluid. Plan B (ORS 75 mL/kg over 4 h) is for some dehydration, not severe. Remember to add zinc for 14 days, continue feeding, and start ORS by mouth as soon as the child can drink.

Paediatric injury patterns that differ from adults
  • Larger head, weaker neck → head injury dominates; cervical injuries are higher (C1–C3) and SCIWORA (spinal cord injury without radiographic abnormality) is a real entity because the paediatric spine is elastic.
  • Compliant thorax → contusion without fracture; tension pneumothorax develops fast.
  • Proportionally larger, less protected solid organs → liver and spleen injuries common; handlebar injuries cause duodenal and pancreatic trauma.
  • Growth plates → Salter–Harris injuries; a "sprain" in a child is often a physeal fracture.
  • Non-accidental injury is part of every paediatric trauma differential. See Unit 15.

11.2 Burns Must

  • Surface area: the adult "rule of nines" is wrong for children — the head is proportionally much larger and the legs smaller. Use a Lund–Browder chart, or the child's own palm-with-fingers ≈ 1% TBSA for scattered burns.
  • Fluid: Parkland formula 3–4 mL × weight (kg) × %TBSA of Ringer's lactate in the first 24 hours, half in the first 8 hours from the time of injury, half over the next 16 — plus routine maintenance fluid (containing glucose) in children, which adults do not need. Titrate to urine output of 1 mL/kg/h.
  • Airway: facial burns, singed nasal hairs, soot in the mouth, hoarseness or stridor mean the airway will swell. Intubate early — waiting makes it impossible.
  • First aid that actually works: cool running water for 20 minutes (up to 3 hours post-burn), remove clothing and jewellery, cover with cling film, keep the child warm. Do not apply ice, toothpaste, ink, oil, turmeric or ghee — all common, all harmful.
  • Analgesia is a priority, not an afterthought. Consider non-accidental injury with clearly demarcated immersion burns, cigarette burns, or a history that does not fit.

11.3 Drowning Good

Drowning is a leading cause of death in Indian children, particularly around open wells, ponds, canals and during floods. Key points: the primary insult is hypoxia, so resuscitation begins with five rescue breaths before compressions; there is no meaningful distinction between "fresh" and "salt" water for initial management; abdominal thrusts to "expel water" are harmful; hypothermia may be protective in children and prolonged resuscitation is justified in cold-water immersion; and all symptomatic children need observation for delayed pulmonary oedema. Prevention — well covers, fencing, supervision, swimming instruction — saves more lives than any hospital intervention.

Q1. A 6-year-old (20 kg) has 25% TBSA scald burns sustained 1 hour ago. Using the Parkland formula at 4 mL/kg/%TBSA, how much Ringer's lactate is required in the first 8 hours from the time of injury, and what else must be prescribed?

1000 mL over the remaining 7 hours, plus maintenance. Total 24-hour requirement = 4 × 20 × 25 = 2000 mL; half (1000 mL) is due within 8 hours of the burn, and one hour has already elapsed, so it runs over the remaining 7. The paediatric-specific point is that children additionally need routine maintenance fluid with glucose because of limited glycogen reserve — a detail routinely missed when adult protocols are applied to children. Titrate to a urine output of 1 mL/kg/h.

Q2. A 7-year-old in rural Maharashtra is brought at 3 a.m. having woken with severe abdominal pain and vomiting. There is no visible bite. Over an hour he develops ptosis and difficulty swallowing. Most appropriate action?

Treat as krait envenomation now. Antivenom dose is not weight-adjusted — a child receives the same amount as an adult because the injected venom load is identical. The commonest cause of death is unrecognised respiratory paralysis, so the airway plan matters as much as the antivenom. Never wait for snake identification.

Almost everything you learned in Units 4–10 changes in a severely malnourished child. Applying standard emergency protocols to them is one of the commonest and most lethal errors in paediatric practice — and it is entirely avoidable.

12.1 Diagnosis Must

Severe acute malnutrition in children 6–59 months = any one of: weight-for-height/length below −3 SD of the WHO standards; mid-upper arm circumference < 11.5 cm; or bilateral pitting oedema of nutritional origin. Presence of any medical complication, an inability to complete an appetite test with ready-to-use therapeutic food, or age under 6 months indicates inpatient care; the rest can be managed in the community.

Refeeding syndrome

When carbohydrate is reintroduced, insulin drives phosphate, potassium and magnesium into cells. Serum levels crash, causing arrhythmia, cardiac failure, rhabdomyolysis, seizures and death — typically on days 2–5 of feeding. This is precisely why WHO starts with the low-energy, low-protein, low-sodium F-75 formula rather than aggressive nutrition. Start low, go slow, supplement thiamine, and monitor electrolytes. The instinct to "feed the starving child properly" is the instinct that kills them.

India lens

India carries a large share of the world's wasted children. Management is structured through Nutrition Rehabilitation Centres (facility-based, following the WHO ten steps) linked to community-level identification through Anganwadi workers, ASHAs and the ICDS, with MUAC tapes as the field screening tool. Two persistent implementation problems are worth knowing: defaulting — families cannot afford weeks of lost wages for an inpatient stay, which is an argument for well-run community-based management of acute malnutrition — and the frequent coexistence of tuberculosis, HIV and severe anaemia, which must be actively excluded rather than assumed absent. A child who is not gaining weight on correct feeding has an undiagnosed infection until proven otherwise.

Complicated SAM — ideal
  • Daily electrolytes including phosphate and magnesium during refeeding, with pre-emptive correction.
  • Prompt exclusion of tuberculosis, HIV and coeliac disease; echocardiography where cardiac failure is suspected.
  • Dietetic and developmental therapy input; structured play programme delivered by trained staff.
Complicated SAM — resource-constrained
  • The WHO ten steps were designed for this setting and need almost no technology. Warmth, F-75, 2–3 hourly feeds including overnight, ReSoMal, potassium and magnesium, empirical antibiotics and no iron is the whole intervention.
  • Without serum phosphate, prevent refeeding syndrome by protocol rather than measurement: start at F-75 energy levels, do not accelerate, and be suspicious of any child who deteriorates on days 2–5 of feeding.
  • MUAC tape and the oedema check are the screening tools — usable by an Anganwadi worker or ASHA with no scales.
  • Defaulting is the main cause of treatment failure, and it is economic, not clinical. Where the child passes the appetite test and has no complication, community-based management with RUTF keeps the family earning and the child treated.
  • Actively exclude tuberculosis and HIV. A child not gaining weight on correct feeding has an undiagnosed infection until proven otherwise.

Q1. A 14-month-old with visible severe wasting, MUAC 10.2 cm, and 3 days of watery diarrhoea is lethargic with sunken eyes and cold hands. Which management step is correct?

ReSoMal, slowly, with close monitoring. Plan C volumes will precipitate cardiac failure in reductive adaptation. Standard ORS has too much sodium and too little potassium for a child who is total-body sodium-overloaded and potassium-depleted. Signs of over-hydration to watch for are an increasing pulse rate, increasing respiratory rate, enlarging liver and periorbital puffiness — stop the fluid if they appear. Intravenous fluid is reserved for shock, and even then given slowly. Also give empirical antibiotics: this child may be septic with no fever and no tachycardia.

Q2. Why is iron withheld during the stabilisation phase of severe acute malnutrition?

Free iron is dangerous in the stabilisation phase. Transferrin and other iron-binding proteins are depleted, so supplemental iron circulates unbound — promoting bacterial proliferation and generating free radicals in a child whose antioxidant defences are already exhausted. Iron is introduced in the rehabilitation phase, once appetite has returned, infection is treated and the child is gaining weight.

13.1 The parent's concern is a vital sign Must

Carer concern independently predicts serious illness and deterioration, and it appears as an escalation trigger in modern early warning systems for exactly that reason. The specific phrases that should raise your index of suspicion:

  • "He is not himself." / "She is not playing."
  • "He has never been like this before."
  • "I have brought him three times and he is getting worse."
  • "She sleeps all the time now." / "He wakes up crying at night with leg pain."

The corollary matters as much: a child who is playing — genuinely playing, not being coaxed — is rarely about to collapse. Put a toy or a phone in front of them and watch.

13.4 Faltering growth and failure to thrive Good

Plot the growth. Always plot the growth. A single weight is nearly meaningless; a trajectory crossing centiles downward is a diagnosis in progress. Structure the differential by mechanism rather than by organ list:

  1. Inadequate intake — poverty and food insecurity, incorrect feed preparation, breastfeeding difficulty, oral aversion, cleft palate, neurological swallowing impairment, neglect, maternal depression. This is the commonest category worldwide and the one most often skipped over.
  2. Inadequate absorption — coeliac disease, cystic fibrosis, cow's milk protein allergy, chronic giardiasis, environmental enteric dysfunction, short bowel.
  3. Increased requirement or loss — congenital heart disease, chronic lung disease, chronic kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, chronic infection (TB, HIV), malignancy.
  4. Impaired utilisation — inborn errors of metabolism, chromosomal and syndromic causes, endocrine disease.

A careful feeding history, an observed feed, a growth chart and a plotted mid-parental height answer more questions than a first-line panel of tests. Investigate because the history points somewhere, not because you are anxious.

13.5 Safety-netting — the skill that catches what you missed Must

Every child sent home without a diagnosis needs an explicit, specific, documented safety net. Vague advice ("come back if worse") does not work. A usable structure:

The four-part safety net
  1. What I think this is, and what I expect to happen — including how long the illness should last.
  2. Exactly what should make you return immediately — named, concrete signs: a rash that does not fade under a glass, breathing fast or sucking in the ribs, refusing all feeds, floppiness or a child you cannot wake, a fit, cold or mottled hands and feet, no urine for 8–12 hours, a fever lasting more than 5 days.
  3. Where to go, and how — the specific facility, the hours, and what to do at 2 a.m. Transport is a clinical variable, not a social one.
  4. A planned review — a definite time and place, not "if needed", when the diagnosis is uncertain.

Then document that you did it, in the words you used. NICE's fever guidance builds this into the traffic-light pathway for a reason: the amber-risk child is discharged safely only if the safety net is real.

Q1. A 5-year-old has had 3 weeks of intermittent fever, has become pale, refuses to walk in the mornings complaining of leg pain, and has a 2.5 cm firm cervical node. Most appropriate next step?

Blood count with a film, urgently. Pallor + prolonged fever + non-mechanical bone pain that limits walking + significant lymphadenopathy is a leukaemia presentation until excluded, and bone pain waking or disabling a child is a specific red flag. A smear examined by a person can show blasts within an hour and costs almost nothing. Growing pains do not cause pallor, fever or morning refusal to walk. Empirical anti-tubercular therapy without investigation in this picture risks treating leukaemia with rifampicin for months.

Q2. Which cervical lymph node feature is least concerning for malignancy in a child?

The tender, mobile, shrinking node. Reactive nodes are tender, mobile, appear with an identifiable infection and regress. The concerning features are size above about 2 cm, hardness, matting, fixation, painlessness, progressive enlargement beyond 2–4 weeks, supraclavicular location at any size, and accompanying B symptoms. In India, persistent nodal enlargement also demands consideration of tuberculous lymphadenitis — and the distinction between lymphoma and TB frequently requires biopsy rather than a therapeutic trial.

14.1 Diagnostic overshadowing Must

The central error

A new symptom in a child with cerebral palsy, Down syndrome, autism or a chronic condition is attributed to the underlying diagnosis and not investigated. The child with cerebral palsy who is "irritable because of spasticity" has a fractured femur, a testicular torsion, a corneal abrasion, a dental abscess, constipation, gastro-oesophageal reflux, or a urinary tract infection. Assume a new symptom has a new cause until you have looked. Non-verbal children signal pain through behaviour change, and the carer who says "this is not his usual irritability" is giving you diagnostic information.

Always ask the carer three questions: What is normal for this child — baseline tone, alertness, saturations, feeding, seizure frequency? What is different today? And what usually works when this happens? The carer of a complex child is frequently the most expert clinician present, and treating them as such is both correct and efficient.

14.4 Ceilings of treatment and advance planning Good PG

For a child with a progressive, life-limiting condition, the most important conversation is the one held before the crisis, in clinic, unhurried. Frame it around what the family hopes for and what they fear, rather than around a list of interventions to be withheld. Document an emergency care plan the family can carry, so that the on-call doctor at 3 a.m. is not making a first-principles decision about a child they have never met.

In the Indian context this must be handled with care: the legal framework for withholding and withdrawing life-sustaining treatment has evolved through Supreme Court judgments on advance medical directives, but institutional practice varies widely, family structures are extended, and decisions are frequently collective rather than individual. Practical guidance: involve the people the family identifies as decision-makers, document discussions contemporaneously and in detail, escalate to your institutional ethics committee where available, and never make a unilateral decision at the bedside under pressure. Paediatric palliative care is an active, skilled intervention — not the absence of treatment.

Q1. A 4-year-old with a tracheostomy is in severe respiratory distress. Oxygen is applied to the tracheostomy but there is no improvement and no chest movement. A suction catheter will not pass. Next step?

Remove and replace the tube. A suction catheter that will not pass means the tube is blocked or displaced, and a blocked tracheostomy is a complete airway obstruction that kills within minutes. Remove it, attempt replacement with the same size, then a smaller size, then a tracheal tube through the stoma. Meanwhile oxygenate both routes — some children have a patent upper airway and can be bag-mask ventilated via the face with the stoma occluded. Imaging and nebulisers have no place in the first minutes.

Q2. A 6-year-old on maintenance chemotherapy arrives with a temperature of 38.6 °C, looking reasonably well. The blood count is not yet back. Correct action?

Antibiotics within one hour, empirically. Febrile neutropenia is a time-critical emergency in which delay is directly associated with mortality, and a well appearance is not protective — neutropenic children mount little inflammatory response and can deteriorate over hours. Take blood cultures peripherally and from any central line, then treat immediately per local protocol. The count informs continuing management; it does not gate the first dose.

Referral, transport, communication and safeguarding

Moving a sick child safely; talking to families; recognising the child who is unsafe at home · ~2.5 h

›

In a referral-dependent health system, the quality of the transfer decision often matters more than any single drug. And the conversation with the parents is not the soft part of the encounter — it is the part they will remember for the rest of their lives.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Decide when to refer, stabilise before transfer, and hand over using a structured tool.
  • UG Communicate serious news and uncertainty to a family, and support family presence during resuscitation.
  • UG Recognise features suggesting child abuse or neglect and know the mandatory reporting obligations in India.
  • PG Lead a resuscitation team, run a debrief, and conduct a duty-of-candour conversation after an adverse event.

15.1 The referral decision Must

Refer while the child is still compensating. The commonest transport disaster is the child transferred after decompensation, in an unequipped vehicle, without a stabilised airway, by staff who cannot manage deterioration en route.

BEFORE ANY TRANSFER — the stabilisation checklist A Airway secure and, if intubated, tube position confirmed and taped; suction available in the vehicle B Oxygen calculated for the journey PLUS a 50% reserve; ventilator/bag checked; saturation monitored C Two working IV/IO lines, secured and visible; fluids and any infusions running on functioning pumps D Glucose checked and corrected; seizures controlled; rescue medication drawn up and labelled E Child WARM — hats, blankets, warm vehicle. Hypothermia during transport is common and harmful ✚ Documentation: notes, investigations, imaging, consent, and the receiving unit's ACCEPTANCE — call and speak to a named person before you send ✚ Parent informed, accompanying where possible, and told exactly where the child is going ✚ Anticipate: what is most likely to go wrong in the next 60 minutes, and is the equipment and drug for it in the vehicle?

Structured handover — ISBAR: Identify (who you are, who the child is) · Situation (what is happening now) · Background (relevant history) · Assessment (your interpretation, including vital signs and weight) · Recommendation (what you want, and by when). Handover is a high-risk moment; do it in a quiet space, with a read-back, and give the receiving team a chance to ask questions.

India lens — transport is the weak link

The 108/102 emergency ambulance services have expanded access substantially, but paediatric-specific equipment, trained paediatric transport personnel and en-route monitoring remain inconsistent, and many transfers still occur in private vehicles or autorickshaws. Practical mitigations within your control: always telephone and secure acceptance before sending; send a written referral note including the weight, drugs given with times, and your contact number; send oxygen if you have any; send a trained escort where at all possible; and give the family a realistic account of what will happen at the other end, including likely costs, so they do not turn back. A referral that the family cannot afford to complete is not a referral.

Moving a sick child — ideal
  • A specialist paediatric retrieval team stabilises the child before travel and transports with full monitoring, ventilation and infusions.
  • Referring clinician's job is early telephone referral, stabilisation to the team's instructions, and complete documentation.
  • Bed and speciality acceptance confirmed electronically; handover structured and recorded.
Moving a sick child — resource-constrained
  • You are the retrieval team. The stabilisation checklist above is not preparation for someone else's work — it is your work, and it must be finished before the vehicle leaves.
  • Telephone and secure acceptance from a named person first. A child arriving unannounced at a full tertiary centre may be turned away, and the family may not survive a second journey financially.
  • Send oxygen if you have any, a trained escort if at all possible, and a written note with the weight, the drugs and their times, and your own phone number.
  • Treat cost and transport as clinical variables. Ask directly whether the family can reach and afford the referral centre. A referral the family cannot complete is not a referral — explore 108/102 ambulance entitlement, government scheme coverage and referral transport support before the family leaves.
  • Keep the child warm en route. Hypothermia during transport is common, harmful and entirely preventable with a hat and a blanket.

15.2 Talking to families Must

Use a structured approach — SPIKES is well-validated and adapts well to paediatrics:

  1. Setting — private, seated, phone silenced, both parents where possible, an interpreter if needed (never a sibling), tissues, no time pressure.
  2. Perception — "What have you been told so far? What is your understanding of what is happening?"
  3. Invitation — "How much would you like me to explain?"
  4. Knowledge — a warning shot ("I'm afraid I have some difficult news"), then plain language, short sentences, no euphemism. Say "died", not "passed" or "we lost him". Use the child's name.
  5. Empathy — stop talking. Allow silence. Name the emotion.
  6. Summary and strategy — what happens next, concretely, and when you will next speak.
Family presence during resuscitation

Offering parents the option to be present during their child's resuscitation is supported by resuscitation guidelines and by the evidence on bereavement outcomes — parents who were present generally report less anxiety and better-adjusted grief, and they do not obstruct care. It requires a designated staff member to stay with them, explain what is happening, and support them. It is an offer, never an obligation. This should be an explicit, rehearsed part of your unit's resuscitation protocol rather than an improvisation.

15.3 Safeguarding Must

Every clinician who sees children will encounter abuse and neglect, and most will miss it at least once. Features that should raise concern:

  • The history: absent, changing, inconsistent between carers, or implausible for the child's developmental stage — a 3-month-old cannot roll off a bed if she cannot roll.
  • Delay in seeking care without adequate explanation.
  • Bruising in a non-mobile infant — bruises in a child who is not yet independently mobile are highly concerning. Bruising of the torso, ears or neck in a child under 4; patterned bruises; bruises in clusters.
  • Burns with sharp demarcation, glove-and-stocking distribution, or cigarette-shaped.
  • Injuries: rib fractures, metaphyseal corner fractures, multiple fractures of differing ages, retinal haemorrhages, unexplained intracranial injury.
  • Neglect: untreated dental disease, faltering growth resolving in hospital, repeated missed appointments, poor immunisation uptake.
  • Fabricated or induced illness: symptoms observed only by one carer, recurrent unexplained presentations, discrepancy between reported severity and objective findings.
  • Sexual abuse: disclosure (always take it seriously), sexually transmitted infection, pregnancy in a minor, anogenital injury, marked behavioural change.
India lens — legal obligations

The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012 creates a mandatory obligation on any person, including a doctor, who has knowledge or apprehension of a sexual offence against a child, to report it to the Special Juvenile Police Unit or local police — failure to report is itself a punishable offence. POCSO also requires that a medical examination of a child be conducted (for a girl child, by a woman doctor) in the presence of a parent or a person the child trusts, and that treatment be provided without insisting on a police requisition or legal formalities first. The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 defines a "child in need of care and protection" and routes cases through the Child Welfare Committee. Additionally, the Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (PCPNDT) Act governs prenatal sex determination. Know your hospital's designated safeguarding lead and the local CWC contact before you need them at midnight — and document meticulously, in the family's own words, with a body diagram.

15.4 Teams, debriefing and candour Good PG

Crisis resource management principles determine whether a resuscitation goes well far more than individual knowledge does: a clearly identified leader who stands back and does not perform procedures; closed-loop communication ("Rahul, give 100 micrograms of adrenaline IV now" → "100 micrograms adrenaline IV, giving now" → "adrenaline given"); role allocation including a scribe and a timekeeper; explicit shared mental models spoken aloud ("this is septic shock, we are on the second bolus, my concern is the airway"); calling for help early; and flattening the hierarchy so the most junior person can voice a concern.

Debrief every significant event — briefly, promptly, and without blame. The PEARLS framework (Promoting Excellence and Reflective Learning in Simulation) works equally well for real clinical debriefing: set the scene and agree confidentiality → gather reactions ("how did that feel?") → analyse using a blend of learner self-assessment, focused facilitation and directive teaching → summarise takeaways and actions. Ten minutes with the whole team, standing, is worth more than a written incident form.

When something goes wrong, tell the family. Say what happened, say you are sorry that it happened, say what you are doing to find out why, and say when you will speak to them again. An apology for an outcome is not an admission of negligence, and families overwhelmingly report that concealment causes more lasting harm than the error itself.

Q1. A 3-month-old presents with a bruise on the left cheek. The parents report she rolled off a sofa. She is not yet rolling on examination. Most appropriate action?

Safeguarding concern. Bruising in an infant who is not independently mobile is highly concerning for inflicted injury, and here the mechanism offered is developmentally impossible — a decisive discrepancy. Full examination, documentation with a body map, coagulation screen, consideration of a skeletal survey and neuroimaging, and escalation to the safeguarding lead are all required. A normal coagulation screen does not exclude abuse and must not be used to close the case.

Q2. Which best describes closed-loop communication during a resuscitation?

Named person, read-back, completion confirmed. Naming the recipient prevents the diffusion of responsibility in which everyone assumes someone else gave the drug; the read-back catches mishearing of doses; and the completion callout keeps the leader's mental model accurate. The leader should generally not perform procedures — a leader with their hands inside a task loses situational awareness, which is the very thing the role exists to provide.

16.1 The know–do gap Good

Audit after audit shows that oxygen is prescribed but not delivered, antibiotics are indicated but given late, weights are estimated rather than measured, observation charts are incomplete overnight, and triage systems exist on paper but not in the corridor. Interventions that reliably narrow the gap are unglamorous and cheap:

  • Pre-calculated, weight-banded emergency drug charts on the resuscitation trolley — eliminating arithmetic at the worst possible moment. Length-based tapes (Broselow-type) work where scales are unavailable.
  • Checklists for intubation, transfer and sepsis bundles.
  • Structured handover at every shift change.
  • Regular in-situ simulation in the actual clinical space — which reliably exposes latent system failures (missing equipment, expired drugs, doors that do not fit a trolley) that classroom teaching never finds.
  • Mortality and near-miss review with a no-blame structure and documented actions.
  • Auditing one measurable behaviour at a time and feeding results back visibly to the team.

The closing argument

Nothing in this module is difficult to understand. Almost all of it is difficult to do reliably at three in the morning, on the fortieth child of the shift, when the oxygen cylinder is empty and the family cannot afford the referral. That is the actual clinical problem. A doctor who has internalised a structured approach, who reassesses, who says out loud what they are worried about, and who builds systems that do not depend on any individual being at their best — that doctor changes outcomes. The algorithm is the easy part.

Q1. A vendor offers your district hospital a machine-learning sepsis prediction tool reporting an AUROC of 0.92 in the developer's dataset. Most appropriate response?

Demand external validation and impact evidence, and check the input requirements. Discrimination in a development dataset says little about performance in a different population, and nothing at all about whether outcomes improve — the same lesson EPOCH taught about PEWS. Then ask the practical question: does your hospital generate continuous electronic vital-sign data at all? A model that cannot be fed is a model that cannot help, however good its AUROC.

Q2. Which is the strongest balancing measure for a project aiming to reduce time-to-antibiotics in suspected paediatric sepsis?

Total antibiotic consumption. A balancing measure detects harm caused by the improvement itself. Pushing hard on speed-to-antibiotics predictably increases antibiotic exposure in children who turn out not to be septic — a real cost in an era of rising resistance. Staff satisfaction and length of stay are useful context but do not capture the specific unintended consequence this change is most likely to produce.

KF-1 · An 8-month-old with fever and lethargy

Stem. An 8-month-old is brought with 2 days of fever and reduced feeding. He is lethargic, has cool peripheries, capillary refill 4 s, HR 180, RR 52, BP 88/54, temperature 39.2 °C. He has a few scattered non-blanching spots on the abdomen.

Q1. List the four most urgent actions in the first 15 minutes. (4 marks)

Q2. Which single physical finding here changes your antibiotic urgency most, and why? (2 marks)

Q3. The child has had 20 mL/kg of saline. HR is 176, liver is now 3 cm and there are bibasal crackles. What is your next step and why? (3 marks)

Model answer. Q1 — high-flow oxygen; IV/IO access within 90 s with blood cultures and bedside glucose; empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics within the hour; titrated fluid (10–20 mL/kg aliquots) with reassessment. Q2 — the non-blanching rash: meningococcal sepsis, antibiotics become immediate rather than merely urgent, and delay is directly associated with death. Q3 — stop further boluses; the emerging hepatomegaly and crackles indicate the limit of fluid tolerance; start a vasoactive infusion (adrenaline) peripherally or via IO while arranging central access and escalate to critical care.

KF-2 · A 4-year-old brought unresponsive

Stem. A 4-year-old from a farming household is brought unresponsive. Pupils 1 mm, copious oral secretions, HR 48, widespread wheeze, muscle twitching. No fever.

Q1. What is the toxidrome and the likely agent? (2 marks)

Q2. State your two therapeutic priorities and the endpoint you titrate the first to. (3 marks)

Q3. Name two actions to protect staff. (2 marks)

Model answer. Q1 — cholinergic toxidrome; organophosphate or carbamate pesticide. Q2 — (i) airway protection and oxygenation, anticipating respiratory failure; (ii) atropine titrated upward until secretions dry and the chest is clear (not to pupil size or heart rate), plus pralidoxime. Q3 — gloves and gown; remove and bag contaminated clothing, decontaminate skin, and ventilate the resuscitation area.

KF-3 · A 10-day-old who "stopped feeding"

Stem. A 10-day-old, born at term at home, is brought with poor feeding since morning. Temperature 35.4 °C, RR 68, HR 190, weak femoral pulses, liver 4 cm, SpO₂ 86% pre-ductal and 76% post-ductal, glucose 2.1 mmol/L.

Q1. Give three differential diagnoses in priority order. (3 marks)

Q2. State four immediate interventions. (4 marks)

Q3. What single infusion may be diagnostic and therapeutic, and what complication must you prepare for? (2 marks)

Model answer. Q1 — duct-dependent congenital heart lesion (differential saturation, weak femorals, hepatomegaly, day 10); neonatal sepsis (hypothermia, poor feeding); metabolic decompensation / inborn error. These are not mutually exclusive — treat for more than one. Q2 — 10% dextrose 2–5 mL/kg for hypoglycaemia then an infusion; warming; oxygen with careful titration; blood cultures then immediate antibiotics; cautious small-volume fluid only. How to run these on almost no budget

None of these scenarios requires a high-fidelity manikin. A basic manikin or even a doll, a set of printed vital-sign cards revealed in sequence, a real drug trolley, and a facilitator willing to voice the parent will achieve most of the learning objectives. Low-cost simulation performs comparably to high-fidelity for many outcomes; what matters is realism of the decisions, not realism of the plastic. Run them in situ — in your actual emergency room — and you will find latent system failures for free.

Scenario 1 · "She's just not feeding" — the 12-day-old

Learning objectives: apply THE MISFITS; recognise duct-dependent circulation; avoid reflexive large-volume fluid; check glucose early.

Stage 1 — 0 min

12-day-old, home delivery, poor feeding for 8 hours. Temp 35.6 °C, HR 188, RR 70, SpO₂ 88% on air, CRT 4 s, mottled. Trigger: does the team check glucose and expose/warm the baby?

Stage 2 — if glucose checked

Glucose 1.9 mmol/L. Expect: 10% dextrose 2–5 mL/kg, not 25% or 50%. If they give 25% or more, the facilitator notes it for debrief rather than interrupting.

Stage 3 — 8 min

Femoral pulses are weak; liver 4 cm; pre-ductal SpO₂ 90%, post-ductal 76%. If the team has given 20 mL/kg or more, the baby's saturations fall and crackles appear. Trigger: do they reconsider the diagnosis?

Stage 4 — resolution

Prostaglandin E1 started → improvement, then apnoea at 4 minutes. Trigger: is the team ready to ventilate?

Debrief focus: anchoring on "sepsis" in a neonate; the value of pre-/post-ductal saturations; why hepatomegaly should stop a bolus; anticipating the complication of your own treatment.

Scenario 2 · The febrile 3-year-old at a CHC without a PICU

Learning objectives: deliver a first-hour sepsis bundle with limited resources; apply health-system-differentiated fluid guidance; make and execute a referral decision.

Stage 1

3-year-old, 2 days of fever, drowsy. HR 172, RR 44, BP 94/60, CRT 4 s, glucose 3.8 mmol/L, temp 39.6 °C. Facilities available: oxygen concentrator, one IV set, ceftriaxone, saline, a glucometer. No ventilator, no inotropes, no PICU.

Stage 2 — decision point

Does the team give 60 mL/kg, or a conservative aliquot approach with immediate antibiotics and referral? Both routes continue; the outcome differs. If 60 mL/kg is given: the child develops respiratory distress and falling saturations at 25 minutes.

Stage 3

The referral hospital is 90 minutes away. The family says they cannot afford the ambulance. Trigger: how does the team handle the social barrier? This is a clinical problem.

Debrief focus: FEAST and its boundaries; what "the cheap things done perfectly" means; the referral checklist; the ethics and practicalities of a family that cannot afford transfer.

Scenario 3 · The convulsing child who will not stop

Learning objectives: execute the time-based status algorithm; resist benzodiazepine stacking; find the cause.

Stage 1

3-year-old convulsing for 8 minutes on arrival. Trigger: airway, oxygen, glucose, timer started, someone calling out elapsed time?

Stage 2

Glucose 5.2. Two doses of midazolam given; the seizure continues. Trigger: if a third benzodiazepine is given, saturations fall and the child becomes apnoeic — the facilitator plays this out.

Stage 3

Sodium returns at 116. The seizure persists despite a second-line agent. Trigger: does the team recognise that hypertonic saline is now the treatment? Background detail available on questioning: the child was admitted yesterday for gastroenteritis and put on 0.45% saline in dextrose.

Debrief focus: the two-dose benzodiazepine ceiling; iatrogenic hyponatraemia and isotonic maintenance fluids; the discipline of a spoken clock; "a seizure that will not stop has a cause you have not found."

Scenario 4 · The child who was sent home yesterday

Learning objectives: revisit a previous diagnosis; recognise cancer red flags; handle a family who feel dismissed; practise disclosure and candour.

Stage 1

5-year-old, fourth presentation in three weeks with fever and leg pain. Previously diagnosed as viral illness and growing pains. Pale, HR 130, a 3 cm cervical node, refusing to weight-bear. The parent is angry. Trigger: does the team re-examine from scratch or accept the prior label?

Stage 2

Full blood count: Hb 6.2 g/dL, WBC 42 × 10⁹/L, platelets 24 × 10⁹/L, blasts on the film.

Stage 3

A trained parent-actor asks: "Why did nobody find this before? We came three times." Trigger: the trainee must respond honestly without blaming colleagues or becoming defensive.

Debrief focus: diagnostic momentum and premature closure; the red-flag cluster; safety-netting that would have shortened the delay; how to be honest about a delayed diagnosis; the emotional load on the clinician.

A 15-MINUTE STRUCTURED DEBRIEF 1. SET THE SCENE (1 min) "This is a learning conversation, not an assessment. What is said here stays here. We assume everyone acted with good intent and did their best with what they knew." 2. REACTIONS (2 min) "In a word or two — how did that feel?" Let everyone speak. Do not analyse yet. 3. DESCRIPTION (2 min) "Can someone summarise what the case was, in 30 seconds?" Establishes a shared factual account. 4. ANALYSIS (8 min) — blend three approaches: ▸ Learner self-assessment: "What went well and why? What would you change?" ▸ Focused facilitation (advocacy–inquiry): "I noticed the third dose of midazolam was given [observation]. I was concerned because of the apnoea risk [advocacy]. What was your thinking at that moment? [inquiry]" ▸ Directive teaching, only for clear knowledge gaps: "Let me clarify the current guidance on ventilation rate, because it changed in 2025." 5. SUMMARY (2 min) Ask each participant for ONE takeaway they will apply. Note any SYSTEM issue found (missing drug, broken equipment, unclear escalation path) and assign an owner and a date. This is how simulation improves a hospital and not just an individual.

C3. Feedback structure

Use R2C2 for longer developmental conversations and Ask–Tell–Ask for quick corridor feedback.

  • R2C2: build Rapport → explore Reactions to the feedback → explore Content (what does the learner make of it?) → Coach for change with a specific, written plan.
  • Ask–Tell–Ask: "How do you think that went?" → give one specific observation and one specific suggestion → "What will you do differently next time?"
Feedback that does not work

The "feedback sandwich" (praise–criticism–praise) is widely taught and poorly supported — learners discount the praise and often miss the message. Be specific, behavioural, timely, limited to one or two points, and always end with an agreed action. And separate feedback from assessment explicitly, or learners will defend rather than reflect.

C5. Setting your own cut score — and why 85% is only provisional

The honest position on the pass mark

The final assessment ships with a cut score of 85%. That number is a provisional standard, not a validated one. In assessment practice a cut score chosen by preference or convention is the weakest defensible option; standards are supposed to be criterion-referenced and set by a formal method with documented judgements. Published comparisons of methods on the same assessment have produced cut scores ranging from about 66% to 86% — which is precisely why the number should not be picked by intuition. If your institution issues this certificate, set the standard properly using one of the methods below and record the judgements. The cut score is adjustable in the module's faculty settings panel.

Method 1 — Modified Angoff (best fit for this written assessment)

  1. Define the borderline candidate in writing, and agree it aloud: "A learner who has completed the module and would just barely be trusted to recognise and begin stabilising a sick child under indirect supervision." Every judgement depends on this shared picture, so do not skip it.
  2. Assemble 6–10 judges who know both the content and the learners — paediatricians, emergency physicians, senior nurses, and at least one person who teaches at district level.
  3. For each item, each judge independently estimates: out of 100 borderline candidates, how many would answer this correctly? Record as a percentage.
  4. Discuss outliers where judges differ by more than about 20 points, then allow a second round of independent estimates. Do not force consensus.
  5. The cut score is the mean of the item means across judges.
  6. Record the borderline definition, the judges, both rounds, and the final figure. That record is the defensibility.

Method 2 — Ebel (useful when items differ markedly in importance)

Judges first classify every item on two axes — relevance (essential / important / desirable) and difficulty (easy / medium / hard) — then estimate the percentage of borderline candidates answering each cell correctly. The cut score is the weighted mean. Ebel suits this module because a "must-know" airway item and a "nice-to-know" item on future directions genuinely should not carry equal weight.

Method 3 — Hofstee (a compromise, and a sanity check)

The group agrees the highest and lowest acceptable cut scores, and the highest and lowest acceptable failure rates. Plotted against the actual score distribution, the intersection gives the cut. Use it as a reality check on an Angoff result that produces an implausible failure rate.

For OSCEs, not written papers

If you are setting a standard for the OSCE stations in Appendix A rather than the written assessment, use borderline regression instead: examiners award both a checklist score and a global rating, and the pass mark is where the regression of checklist on global rating crosses the borderline grade. It is the current default for performance-based assessment because it uses real candidate performance rather than hypothetical judgements. See McKinley & Norcini, AMEE Guide No. 85.

C6. Programme evaluation

Evaluate the module itself against the Kirkpatrick levels, and be honest that most educational evaluation stops too early:

  • Level 1 — Reaction: learner satisfaction survey. Easy, and nearly meaningless on its own.
  • Level 2 — Learning: pre/post MCQ and OSCE scores; skills retention at 3 and 6 months (expect decay without refreshers).
  • Level 3 — Behaviour: audit of actual practice — time to antibiotics, documentation of triage category, glucose checked in altered children, weight recorded.
  • Level 4 — Results: unplanned escalations, early in-hospital mortality, transfer quality. Slow, confounded, and the only level that ultimately matters.

Most digital medical education is designed by preference and defended by anecdote. This appendix exists so that every structural choice in this module can be argued with — which requires first stating what each choice rests on.

G1. Design decisions and their evidence

Design decisionWhat it does hereEvidence / framework
Mastery measured, not completion No "mark as read" control exists. A unit turns green only when every checkpoint item is currently answered correctly. Reading earns nothing. Taylor et al. (2023) found that self-directed learning in health professions education is overwhelmingly assessed by internally developed perception surveys (49.6% of 141 studies) rather than by performance — the core weakness this design corrects. Certification on three independent forms of evidence The certificate requires coverage (all 16 unit checkpoints mastered), retention (≥12 of the 16 units evidenced by an item answered correctly again ≥24 h after first pass) and applied performance (a closed-book integrated assessment at or above the cut score). Where this design is weaker than it looks

Stated plainly, because the brief asks for evidence governance rather than evidence decoration. (1) The retention schedule uses fixed Leitner intervals, not an individually fitted forgetting curve — defensible, not optimal. (2) Two checkpoint items per unit is a thin sample from which to declare mastery; a real certification decision needs the workplace-based assessments in Appendix A alongside it. (3) The adaptive placement is rule-based and self-reported rather than performance-based — a true diagnostic pre-test is the obvious next iteration. (4) None of this has been evaluated in learners: Kirkpatrick levels 3 and 4 for this module are unmeasured, and Appendix C sets out how a department could measure them.

On the certificate specifically: (5a) the retention criterion is a floor, not a stringent test. For a learner working through 28 notional hours over three weeks it is close to automatic, because Part A items will have surfaced repeatedly by the time Part E is reached. Its real function is to make a two-day binge insufficient by requiring at least one return visit on a later day. The certificate therefore also prints the longest interval at which retention was demonstrated, so a reader can distinguish a learner who returned over months from one who returned once. Raising the bar further was considered and rejected: feasibility and acceptability are Ottawa 2018 criteria, and an unattainable certificate simply goes unclaimed. (5b) The 85% cut score is provisional and unvalidated until a local standard-setting panel replaces it — Appendix C carries the worksheet. (6) A 52-item bank sampling 40 gives limited form-to-form equivalence between attempts; a bank of 150+ would be needed for that claim to hold. (7) The assessment is unproctored and open to collusion or lookup, so it measures what the learner can do with their conscience, not under observation — the honest mitigation is the printed scope statement, not a technical one. (8) The verification code is a deterministic hash computed on the learner's own device: it lets an institution re-derive the code from the same inputs, and nothing more. It is not tamper-proof and must not be presented as such. Any high-stakes use of this certificate should be accompanied by institutional invigilation and record-keeping.

A module that claims to be evidence-governed has to be willing to publish its own weaknesses.
