## 1 · Substack post

**Title:** The child who is about to crash looks fine on the monitor

**Subtitle:** I built a free course on the approach to the sick child. It refuses to give you credit for reading it.

A child who dies in hospital usually does so within twenty-four hours of arriving, from a short list of physiological end-points, after a period in which somebody could have noticed. That sentence is the reason this exists.

I have spent a long time watching capable, hard-working doctors miss the compensating child. Not through ignorance — they could recite the algorithm — but because a heart rate of 168 in a febrile toddler with a normal blood pressure does not *feel* like an emergency, and because nobody had ever made them practise the recognition until it was automatic. The knowledge was there. The retrieval was not.

So I built a module that attacks that gap directly.

**It measures mastery, not consumption.** There is no "mark as complete" button anywhere in it. A unit turns green when you answer its checkpoint correctly, and only then. You get no credit for opening it, scrolling it, or feeling that you knew that already.

**It tests you again, later.** Questions come back on a spaced schedule — one day, three days, a week, three weeks, two months — interleaved across topics rather than blocked by unit. Get one wrong and it returns sooner. This is the single best-evidenced study behaviour in medical education and almost no course actually implements it.

**It teaches two medicines.** Where management genuinely differs, you get parallel panels: what you would do with a PICU behind you, and what you would do at a CHC with an oxygen concentrator, no ventilator and a family who cannot afford the referral. Neither is a footnote to the other. The FEAST trial and the health-system-differentiated Surviving Sepsis recommendations exist precisely because that distinction is a clinical one, not a consolation prize.

**It argues with its own guidelines.** EPOCH showed that early warning scores did not reduce mortality — and the module says so, then explains why that finding should not be imported across a resource gradient. Only three of the sixty-one statements in the 2026 paediatric sepsis guidelines rest on moderate-or-better evidence, and the module says that too. Learning to ask *what is the current edition and what changed* is a competency. A textbook cannot teach it.

Sixteen units, roughly twenty-eight hours, from why children die through to future directions. Assessment bank, twelve OSCE stations, four simulation scenarios with debriefing scripts, faculty session plans and rubrics, CBME mapping, and an emergency drug annex that is never locked behind a quiz — because clinical safety material should not be.

There is a certificate at the end. It requires three separate kinds of evidence, not one score, and it prints on its face exactly what it does and does not attest. It does not attest that you can resuscitate a child. Nothing written can.

It is free, it works offline on a phone, it collects nothing about you, and there is no account to create.

## 2 · LinkedIn

I have released a free, self-paced module on the **approach to the sick child** — built for Indian and other resource-constrained settings.

What makes it different from the usual online course:

→ **Mastery, not completion.** No "mark as read" button. A unit counts only when you answer its checkpoint correctly.
→ **Spaced retrieval.** Questions return over 60 days, interleaved across topics. Cramming does not survive it.
→ **Dual-track content.** Ideal setting and resource-constrained setting shown as equal parallel panels — a PICU protocol and a district hospital protocol, side by side.
